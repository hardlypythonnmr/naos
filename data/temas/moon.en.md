## 1 · The tale {#the-tale}

History · Sources [1]

I had this one from the Met Office's own history of it. That's the British weather service.

On the night of 25 October 1859, a ship called the Royal Charter went down in a storm off Anglesey. More than 450 people died.

After that, a man named FitzRoy began sending storm warnings to the coast. The first one went out in February 1861. The warnings reached the radio in 1924, and the BBC in 1925.

Today it's called the Shipping Forecast. The Met Office writes it, and BBC radio reads it out, sea area by sea area, all 31 of them.

We borrowed only its shape, for the moon. The first report says:

"Moon. Almost full. Full tomorrow night. Eat one meal where you can see it."

This year, in Medellín, the moon is full on that same date, the 25th, at 23:12.

Two weeks later, the report says less:

"Moon. Dark. Tonight the moon is not there."

## 2 · On a moon night {#tonight}

Our experiment

On any of the five nights below, or one close to it. About ten minutes, after dark.

1. **Check the night.** Full, or new (the dark one): the column tells you which, and at what hour.
2. **Step out, or eat where you can see it.** With one person or on your own: a balcony, a doorway, somewhere safe. On a full night it comes up around sunset. On a dark night there's nothing to find. Go out anyway. [5]
3. **Stay one minute and ask for nothing.** No wish, no plan. One breath in, one long breath out. Then you go back in. That's it.

If you pray, make a wish or light a candle on these nights, as many families do on Velitas, keep doing it; our minute can come after. [17]

## 4 · Where this comes from {#where-this-comes-from}

(Folded. Opens on tap.)

### The report

The form is the Shipping Forecast's. The Met Office tells how it grew out of FitzRoy's storm warnings after the Royal Charter was lost on the night of 25 October 1859, with more than 450 lives. The first warning went out in February 1861, radio came on 1 January 1924, and the BBC in July 1925. The Met Office writes it for the Maritime and Coastguard Agency, BBC radio reads it, and it covers 31 sea areas. We took only the shape: a short report, read the same way each time. No audio, no music and no announcer's voice. [1]

### The moon itself

A full moon rises around sunset and stays up all night. A new moon rises and sets with the Sun, so it can't be seen. [5] [2]

A full moon looks full for about three days around the exact moment. That's why each report comes the night before. [5] [4]

The column at the top gives the hour of each full and new moon, in both cities. The distance chart shows how the full moons get closer.

### The same dark moon, in another calendar {#hanukkah}

In the Jewish calendar, Hanukkah runs eight days from the 25th of Kislev, with one more light each night. This year it starts on Friday 4 December, with the first candle lit before sundown, and lasts through Saturday 12 December. On 7 December, the fourth candle. The new moon of 8 December falls inside those eight days. [12] [13] [14] [2]

### Where they disagree {#where-they-disagree}

**Velitas: when.** The bishops: the night of the 7th and the early hours of the 8th. In Barranquilla, El Heraldo reports that people light them in the early hours of the 8th, while inland they are usually lit on the night of the 7th. Inland, on the night of the 7th; on the Caribbean coast, many wait for the small hours of the 8th. [15] [18]

**The extra place at Wigilia.** Three readings, each with its owner. A scholar at the University of Łódź: it is for the dead, who may visit as a stranger, a wanderer, a beggar. Busted Halo, a Catholic magazine, writing from inside the custom: it is set for Christ, "the unexpected guest". Muzeum Polonii at the University of Warsaw: a sign of hospitality and of remembering loved ones who are absent. [24] [25] [26]

**How many supermoons.** Sky at Night counts three in 2026: January, November and December. By Nolle's rule, within 90% of the closest approach "in a given orbit", January's comes in or drops out depending on which orbit you take. Three by most counts. [10] [9] [8]

**How much bigger.** NASA says a supermoon can look up to 14% bigger and 30% brighter than the faintest full moon of the year. In 2026 that faintest one was on 31 May. Against an ordinary full moon the difference is smaller. [8] [4]

**Does the moon change us?** One field study found people fell asleep later and slept less on the nights before a full moon. Another sleep study found no link, and an older review of studies found none for behavior. Psychiatric emergency visits in France, 2016 to 2023, showed no link either. A small, contested effect on sleep, and none found elsewhere. [32] [33] [34] [35]

### The sign behind the tap {#the-sign}

The sign on each card is the Western tropical zodiac: twelve signs of 30 degrees, counted from the March equinox, separate from the constellations of the same names. The sidereal zodiac used in India, in its Lahiri form, sits about 24 degrees behind, so its sign is usually a different one. We give the sign the way the astrological tradition holds it. Controlled tests of astrology, like a double-blind study in Nature in 1985, have not found the effects it claims. [27] [28]

### Study (about the behavior)

Nearby studies, none about the moon: a weekly awe walk [30] and empathetic calls from trained callers [31].

- In an 8-week trial with 60 older adults, a weekly 15-minute walk spent looking for wonder was linked to more warm, social feelings and less distress day to day. Anxiety and depression did not change. That was a walk; nobody studied the moon. [30]
- In a 4-week trial with 240 homebound adults aged 27 to 101, most of them 65 or older, short, empathetic phone calls from trained callers lowered loneliness. Those were calls. [31]
- Eating with others is linked to better mood in older adults and better nutrition in children. We found no trial that tests sharing a table by itself on mood. [29]

### Our experiment

Stepping out on the moon nights, the meal where you can see the moon, the minute that asks for nothing, the call under the same moon, the extra place: no study stands behind these. We are trying them.

### How we checked this page

On 16 October 2026 we opened every source above, recomputed the moon against USNO and NASA JPL Horizons to the minute and to the tenth of a percent, and checked each study for retractions (none). For each religious or astrological tradition, an official or high-reputation source guides the reading, and what we say about it is crossed with at least three sources, at least one from inside. For the sign, the figure of about 24 degrees rests on the 1956 Lahiri value that Astrodienst gives, a Vedic table computed with the same ephemeris, and our own calculation; those three are not fully independent. The moon figures come from USNO and NASA JPL Horizons, which agree to the minute. The history of the Shipping Forecast comes from the Met Office, which writes it. Where sources disagree, we say so above.

### What we could not confirm

- The months that open with these new moons in the Chinese, Hebrew and Islamic calendars: we had one or two sources for each, not three, so they aren't on this page.
- That candles were lit around the world on the night of 1854: the bishops tell it, and we found no historical record. It stays as their telling.
- The Novena de Aguinaldos, 16 to 24 December: no source we read. Left out.
- The bishops' original note on Velitas: we read it through Catholic.net and ACI Prensa; the Conference's own site didn't give it.
- The text of Colombia's Law 51 of 1983: the official pages didn't load. Telemedellín confirms the Tuesday holiday.
- The uposatha, the Theravada days of observance at new and full moon: we have two sources and not yet a third independent one, so it isn't on this page.
- The names Cold Moon and Long Night Moon, credited by an almanac to the Mohawk and Mohican peoples: we found no source from those peoples, so we don't use the names.
- "Luna Fría" and "Luna del Cazador" in Spanish: no source on how people use them.
