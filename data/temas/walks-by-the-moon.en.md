## 1 · The tale {#the-tale}

Our tale

This tale is ours. The oldest thing in it is a farmer's calendar: Hesiod, a Greek poet, counted the days of the growing month and said which were good for work. [1] [2]

In our tale, an old farmer watched the moon grow. Every evening, from the night it was half lit until it was round, he walked the same road.

A girl from the next farm watched him go by. On the second evening she asked:

"Where does it go?"

"Nowhere. Out to the bend and back."

"Then why walk it again?"

"Walk it with me tomorrow," he said. "Then tell me."

She did, and the next evening, and the next. The fence was the same fence. The dog at the gate was the same dog. The moon over the hill had grown a little.

On the last evening it was round, and the road was white under it.

"Well?" he asked.

She looked at the road for a while.

"Tomorrow?" she said.

### The same road {#the-same-road}

From the half moon to the full moon: about a week of evenings. Ten minutes is plenty. Next half moons: Tue 17 Nov and Thu 17 Dec. [4]

1. **Pick one road.** One you know and feel fine on in the evening. Out to a bend and back is plenty.
2. **Stop at the same spot and look up.** Phone in your pocket. Find the moon and see how much of it is lit.
3. **Mark it.** One pencil mark on a card for each evening you walked. Stop when the moon is full.

If it's cloudy, walk anyway. If you miss an evening, walk the next one. If you roll instead of walking, roll it.

In one line: when I finish dinner, I walk my road and look for the moon.

Watch the first walk → Eight evenings, one road

That's it. You don't have to learn anything.

### Go see the Geminids {#geminids}

Sun 13 Dec, ten at night, your local time.

Plan on at least 20 minutes outside, and dress for the cold.

1. **Go with someone, to a place you know.** Away from street lights if you can. Dressed warm.
2. **Turn the lights off and wait.** About 20 minutes for your eyes to adjust. Phone dark; if you need light, make it red.
3. **Look up and call out each one.** Face different directions. Say "there" at every meteor.

In New York and Medellín, a thin crescent has set before ten, so it won't get in your way.

In one line: when it's ten on 13 December, I'm outside in my coat, looking up.

That's it.

### Walk side by side {#side-by-side}

Tue 15 Dec, an evening. Ten minutes.

1. **Ask one person.** "Ten minutes, around the block?" Say when and where.
2. **Walk next to them, facing the same way.** Your own pace. Let the steps fall as they fall.
3. **Turn halfway and come back.** Talk, or don't.

In one line: when the day winds down, I ask one person: "Ten minutes?"

That's it.

### Walk home the long way {#the-long-way}

Thu 17 Dec. Five minutes more than usual.

The moon is just past half, high at dusk.

1. **Start home the usual way.** At the first corner, take another street you know and feel fine on.
2. **Stop once and look up.** Find the moon.
3. **Look for one thing you've never noticed.** A roof, a tree, a door.

In one line: when I leave for home, I take the long way.

That's it.

### If you're on your own {#on-your-own}

For the Geminids: watch from a doorstep, a balcony or a window with the lights off. If you go farther, tell one person where you are.

For walking side by side: if there's no one to ask today, walk where other people walk, a park path or a market street, and say hello to one person. Or phone someone far away, and each of you walks your own road.

If the night weighs on you, the help lines are at the bottom of this page.

## 4 · Where this comes from {#where-this-comes-from}

(Folded. Opens on tap.)

The tale and the walks are ours. What we lean on is borrowed: a Greek farmer's calendar, the phases of the moon and, behind a tap, the sign the astrological tradition gives the moon. For the calendar, the Greek text and a published study of the Greek calendar guide the reading, and Hellenion, an organization of Hellenic Polytheists, speaks from inside. For the sign, the documentation of Astrodienst's Swiss Ephemeris guides, with a Western and a Vedic view named.

### Study (about walking) {#study}

- **Nearby studies, none about the moon.** We found no study of walking by moon phase (searched on 16 October 2026), and none of the studies below tested walking with the moon. There are studies of physical activity and the moon in children and teenagers, and their results are mixed. [23]
- **For the long way home.** In an 8-week trial with 60 healthy older adults, a weekly 15-minute "awe walk" (looking for wonder on the way) was linked to more awe, joy and warm social feelings, and to a bigger drop in daily distress, than ordinary walks. Anxiety and depression did not change in either group. [16]
- **On low mood, in general, not tied to these walks.** A review of 75 randomized trials of walking (8,636 people; 68 measured depression) found fewer depressive symptoms in adults who walked than in adults in inactive comparison groups (such as no intervention, a waiting list or usual care; 44 trials). Against adults given another activity (17 trials), it found no clear difference. The authors flag small-study effects, and most trials were rated unclear or high risk of bias. The programs ran from 10 days to 18 months; none was as short as a week. [12]
- A Cochrane review of exercise for adults with depression (73 trials) found lower depression scores with exercise, with low certainty. It covers exercise in general, not walking in particular, and long-term follow-up was rare. None of this replaces treatment. [13]

### Official guidance {#guidance}

Official guidance counts walking. The CDC advises adults to get at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and the NHS says a brisk 10-minute daily walk counts toward it (for ages 19 to 64). The WHO lists walking, cycling and wheeling, at any level of skill. None of them mentions the moon. [24]

### Our experiment {#our-experiment}

The same road for a week, the pencil marks, the phone in your pocket, the ten minutes, the person beside you, the long way home: no study we'd stand behind. What there is:

- **The road.** In a study of 96 people who repeated a daily behavior in the same context, such as after breakfast (82 gave enough data), it took from 18 to 254 days for the behavior to become automatic, and missing one day did not matter much. It did not compare the same place with changing places. A theory paper by Wood and Neal ties habits to the places where they happen. The choice of one road is ours. [21] [22]
- **Side by side.** Researchers have suggested that walking side by side, with a shared direction and a shared view, might help people talk through disagreements. They also write that little is known about it, and we found no study that compares it with sitting face to face. [17] In another study, pairs walked side by side in silence: a better first impression went with steps that fell more in time. It was an association, with no face-to-face group. [18]
- **Walking groups.** A review of 42 studies measured change from the start to the end of walking-group programs: lower blood pressure (about 3.7 and 3.1 mm Hg), lower resting heart rate (about 2.9 beats a minute) and lower depression scores. A group is more than two people on a pavement, and the review cannot say the walking caused the change. A second review, of 19 studies, linked walking groups to more physical activity, with signs of publication bias. [14] [15]
- **In step.** Three experiments found more cooperation after people acted in sync. [19] A later, preregistered test found that people who only read about those studies expect the same effects, and that repeat studies with extra controls failed to find the original ones. [20] So we don't promise that your steps will match or that trust will follow.

### Tradition: an old farmer's calendar {#the-calendar}

*Tradition.* Hesiod's *Works and Days* is a poem about farm life in ancient Greece. Near its end it lists the days of the month. Of the eighth and ninth days of the growing month, it says they are especially good for the work people do. We paraphrase it here and don't quote it, and we don't present it as scripture. [1]

Two things Hesiod doesn't say. He says nothing about walking. And in these lines he never names the moon: his word is "growing" month. [1]

Today, Hellenion, an organization of Hellenic Polytheists, keeps a monthly calendar based on that of the ancient Athenians, counted from the first visible crescent; it calls the Noumenia "the likely first day of the visible New Moon". [3]

On that count, the first quarter, on 18 October, falls near the seventh and eighth days of a Greek month. Hellenion's 2026 calendar puts the eighth day on 19 October. [3] [4]

### Where they disagree {#where-they-disagree}

**Which day is the eighth.** Hesiod, in Evelyn-White's translation, puts the eighth and ninth days in the waxing month, and the Greek says "growing". Hellenion's calendar counts day one from the first visible crescent, so its day eight is 19 October. One talks about work, the other about festivals. The count changes. [1] [3]

**Whether the calendar follows the moon.** Evelyn-White's note says the month's three parts answer to the phases of the moon. Thomson, in a study of the Greek calendar, writes that in this last section of the poem, whose days all began in the lunar phases, "there is no mention of the moon". We say "the growing month". [1] [2]

The sources on the sky and on walking also differ here and there; we list where in section 8, under "How the sources differ".

### The sky {#the-sky}

*Measured.* The first round: the moon was at first quarter on 18 October at 16:12 UTC (12:12 in New York, 11:12 in Medellín) and full on 26 October at 04:12 UTC: Sunday 25 October at 23:12 in Medellín, Monday 26 October at 00:12 in New York. That is 7 days and 12 hours. Counting calendar days from the first quarter to the day of the full moon gives 9 in New York (18 to 26 October) and 8 in Colombia (18 to 25). [4] [5]

Every round works the same. The next ones: first quarter on Tuesday 17 November at 11:48 UTC (06:48 in New York and Medellín) and full on Tuesday 24 November at 14:53 UTC (09:53 in both cities, so the last evening is the 23rd); then first quarter on Thursday 17 December at 05:42 UTC (00:42 in both cities) and full on 24 December at 01:28 UTC, which is 20:28 on Wednesday 23 December in both cities. [4] [5]

The Geminids are active from 4 to 17 December and peak on the night of 13 to 14 December. The American Meteor Society calls them usually the strongest shower of the year, and the one big shower with good activity before midnight. On 13 December the moon is a thin waxing crescent that sets at 20:43 in New York and 21:42 in Medellín, so it is gone before ten; the AMS calls the lunar conditions favorable, with limited moonlight interference. [6] [7] [8] [4] [5]

### The sign behind the tap {#the-sign}

The sign on each card is the Western tropical zodiac: twelve signs of 30 degrees, counted from the March equinox, separate from the constellations of the same names. The sidereal zodiac used in India, in its Lahiri form, sits about 24 degrees behind, so its sign is usually a different one: on 22 October, Aquarius rather than Pisces. We give the sign the way the astrological tradition holds it. Controlled tests of astrology, like a double-blind study in Nature in 1985, have not found the effects it claims. [5] [25] [26] [27] [28]

### How the sources differ {#how-sources-differ}

**When to watch the Geminids.** The AMS: good activity before midnight, with Gemini well placed from 22:00. NASA: it starts around 9 or 10 pm. EarthSky: the radiant is highest around 2 am. We ask for ten, and don't call it the best hour. [6] [7] [8]

**How many meteors.** NASA: 40 to 50 an hour under dark skies, up to 120 in perfect conditions. EarthSky: 120 at best and 50 or more on a dark night. The AMS gives no rate. We give none. [6] [7] [8]

**What the Geminids come from.** They come from 3200 Phaethon. NASA: "an asteroid or a possible rock comet". EarthSky: a dormant or extinct comet. The AMS: an asteroid. [6] [7] [8]

**Moonlight on 13 December.** The AMS says about 15% lit, our measurement 18% to 21%. They are different hours. We say "a thin crescent" and the hour it sets. [6] [4] [5]

**Whether a group helps with low mood.** The 2024 review of walking says in one sentence that walking in a group did not lower depression compared with inactive comparison groups, and in another that walking helped in both formats, in a group or alone. Its table gives the group subgroup (5 trials) a difference that is not significant. We don't use the review for walking with others. [12]
