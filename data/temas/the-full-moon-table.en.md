## 1 · The tale {#the-tale}

Tradition · Held by the tradition · Sources [1] [2] [5] [6]

I had this one from Hindu calendars and temples. It falls on the full moon of Ashvin, at the end of the monsoon rains.

That night, in homes and temples, a bowl of kheer goes outside. Rice cooked in milk and sugar. It stays in the moonlight until morning.

The tradition holds that the moon is full in all sixteen of its parts, the kalas, and that its light carries amrita, a nectar. The kheer gathers it.

In the morning the bowl comes back in, and everyone eats from it. Now it's prasad, food offered first and then shared.

In Bengal, Odisha and Assam the same night has another name, Kojagari, after a question in Sanskrit. Many families there stay up. They say Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth, comes into the world that night and asks:

"Who is awake?"

So somebody keeps watch.

Not every family leaves the same thing out. Not every calendar marks the same night. I'll tell you that part further down.

## 2 · Tonight {#tonight}

Our experiment

There, somebody keeps watch over the bowl. Here, you can keep a plate for someone.

Sunday 25 October, or any night this week. One meal, as long as dinner takes.

1. **Set two plates.** Before you know who's coming.
2. **Take your plate where you can see the sky.** On Sunday the full moon comes up low in the east, around sunset. Put your phone face down.
3. **Before the first bite, look up.** One breath in, one out. Then eat. Talk, or be quiet together.

Think of one person, near you or a call away, and ask: want to eat together? On a call, put it on speaker. If they can't make it, the second plate waits. [Eating alone tonight?](#on-your-own)

Fasting isn't part of this. Nor is leaving food out overnight. Your plate is for tonight's dinner.

In one line: "When I set the table on Sunday, I set one more plate. I look up first."

That's it.

## 4 · Where this comes from {#where-this-comes-from}

### The night

Sharad Purnima is the full moon of the month of Ashvin, in sharad, the autumn after the monsoon. It has other names: Kojagari, Kaumudi, Raas Purnima. [1] [2] [3] [4]

BAPS Swaminarayan temples tell of Lakshmi asking who is awake. Drik Panchang describes the night vigil, and the Lakshmi Puja kept in those same three regions. [2] [1] [6]

For many Vaishnava devotees it is the night of the rasa lila, Krishna's circle dance with the gopis of Vraja. ISKCON London holds an evening of darshan and kirtan, devotional singing, on Sunday 25 October, 18:00 to 21:30 London time. [3] [2] [9]

The Bhagavata Purana (10.29) places the rasa on autumn nights, under a full moon that rises reddish. It does not name the month. That it is this night is held by the tradition. [7]

Many keep a fast that day. We don't ask anyone to fast. [1] [5]

### Where they disagree {#where-they-disagree}

**The date.** Drik Panchang puts the night on Sunday 25 October in Delhi, New York and Medellín. In Mountain View, California, it puts it on Saturday 24, because there the full-moon day begins at 23:25 on the 24th. In India, Valmiki Jayanti, the birthday of Maharishi Valmiki, the sage who composed the Ramayana, comes from the same full moon and falls on Monday 26, by the sunrise rule: the day that dawns inside the full-moon day. The date depends on where you are and which rule your family keeps. [1] [8] [10]

**Whose night.** For Kojagari in Bengal, Odisha and Assam, it is Lakshmi's night. For ISKCON, and for BAPS alongside Lakshmi's question, it is the night of Krishna's rasa. [1] [2] [3]

**Which rasa.** Drik Panchang and BAPS put the Maha Raas on this full moon. ISKCON Mayapur also keeps a Rasa Yatra on the full moon that ends the month of Kartik, a month later. In Odisha that later full moon is Rasa Purnima, on Tuesday 24 November 2026. [1] [2] [12] [13]

**What is set out that night.** Drik Panchang: kheer, left in the moonlight. BAPS: dudh-poha, flattened rice soaked in milk, offered to God and then shared. Maharashtra's tourism office: in some regions, a vessel of boiling milk, where people look at the moon's reflection. [1] [2] [11]

**The light.** Drik Panchang says the moon's rays that night have healing properties and fortify the kheer, and Maharashtra's tourism office says the same. We found no study that measured it, so we leave that claim with them. [1] [11]

### A second root {#a-second-root}

Seneca, a Stoic, wrote to his friend Lucilius around the years 62 to 65 and said he had to borrow this one from Epicurus: [19] [20]

"Look first at whom you eat and drink with, before what you eat and drink. A feast without a friend is the life of a lion and a wolf." [19] [21] [22]
Our translation from the Latin.

Epicurus counted friendship as by far the greatest of the goods wisdom brings to a happy life. [23]

The two roots are kept apart here. Neither one explains the other.

### Study (about the behavior)

- In 142 countries, people who share more lunches and dinners report higher life satisfaction. An association. [15]
- In a trial with 240 homebound adults in 2020, four weeks of friendly phone calls lowered loneliness on one scale, with no clear change on another. Nobody shared a meal in that trial. [16]
- In 24 studies, eating while distracted went with eating more at the next meal. Paying closer attention during the meal, on its own, showed no clear effect. [17]
- We found no trial that tests sharing a table by itself on mood or loneliness. The trials we found also changed the food. [14] [18]
- Older adults who ate alone were more likely to be depressed (odds ratio 1.58, from 1.33 to 1.87, across 8 studies). All of them measured people at a single moment, so they can't say which causes which. [14]

### Our experiment

The second plate set before anyone says yes, the phone face down, the look up before the first bite: no study stands behind these. We are trying them anyway.

### What we could not confirm

- Spiced milk for Kojagiri in Maharashtra: only in recipes and an encyclopedia. The state's own page describes something else, so we left it out.
- That Gaudiya temples in Bengal favor the Kartik rasa over this one: the pages we were pointed to did not open.
- Schweig's study of the rasa (Dance of Divine Love, Princeton, 2005): not read yet.
- India's central government holiday list for 2026: not read. We used NITTTR Chandigarh's.
- A temple page says this is the night the Moon is nearest to Earth. We did not check it, so we don't repeat it.
- The phone face down and the pause before the first bite: no study we would stand behind.
- The text of Colombia's Law 51 of 1983: the official pages did not load. Radio Nacional confirms the Monday holiday.
