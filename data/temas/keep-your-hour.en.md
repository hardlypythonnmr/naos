# The clock moved. Keep your hour.

What do you do with an hour that happens twice?

A tale we made up, and one thing to try for seven nights: keep your bedtime.

[You came from: {title of the piece}]

## 1 · The tale {#the-tale}

Our tale · The night is real [1] [2]

This tale is ours. The night in it is real: once a year, in most of the United States, the clocks go back and one hour happens twice.

In our tale, a town kept a big clock in its tower, and a woman turned its hands. Every year, on the first Sunday of November, she climbed up at 2:00 in the morning.

One year a boy climbed after her, in his pajamas.

"You're turning time back," he said.

"Giving the town its hour back." She set the hands to 1:00. "There. It happens twice."

"Does the sun know?"

"Never. I move the hands. I have never moved the sun."

"So when do I go to bed tomorrow?"

She looked at him over her glasses. "When did you go to bed yesterday?"

"Ten."

"Then ten."

The next evening, when the tower struck ten, his light was already out.

## 2 · In the evening {#in-the-evening}

Our experiment · the breathing comes from a longer version that was studied [23]

What the boy did, on your own evening. About two minutes, then seven nights.

1. **Pick your hour.** The time on the clock when you went to bed before it moved. If it changed from night to night, pick one you can keep. Write it where you'll see it.
2. **Close the day on purpose.** Breathe in, then in a little more, then out, long and slow. That's one breath. Take three. If you get dizzy, go back to normal breathing.
3. **Go to bed at your hour, and mark it.** Write down when you got into bed. Get up at a similar time each day. Seven nights.

If a night goes differently, write that hour down too, and go on with the next one.

Why: the NHS advises going to bed and getting up at similar times each day [7]. Nobody has tested keeping your bedtime through the clock change.

If you want it in one line: when the clock says my hour, I take three breaths and turn off the light.

That's it. You don't have to learn anything.

If trouble sleeping lasts for months, or gets in the way of daily life, see a doctor.

### If your clock didn't move {#your-clock-didnt-move}

Colombia keeps the same hour all year, UTC minus 5. Hawaii and most of Arizona also keep theirs all year. If that is you, nothing moved, and the steps stay the same. Your hour is already yours. Keep it.

## 4 · Where this comes from {#where-this-comes-from}

(Folded. Opens on tap.)

There is no tradition behind this page. The tale is ours, and we quote no sacred text. What stands behind the behavior is advice from health services and studies, and where they disagree we say so. The one old tradition near the page is the Moon's sign, in the last part of this block.

### Advice (about the behavior)

- The NHS advises going to bed and getting up at similar times each day: it helps your body know when it is time to sleep. [7]
- The American Academy of Sleep Medicine puts the stress on the morning: "Get up at the same time every day, even on weekends or during vacations." [9]
- Spain's sleep society calls a regular sleep-wake rhythm one of the fundamental pillars of health (our translation). [10]

### Study (about the hour)

Every figure below is an association. None of it shows that keeping your bedtime changes anything.

- Across many studies, steadier sleep and wake times go with better health: fewer depressive symptoms, lower BMI, less high blood pressure, fewer cardiovascular events. The evidence is observational and its quality runs from very low to moderate. We read the abstracts of all three; the Kalkanis review is behind a paywall. [14] [15] [16]
- In a UK study of 88,975 adults who wore a wrist sensor for 7 days, people at the irregular end of the scale (the 5th percentile) had 1.53 times the risk of dying over about 7 years, compared with a typical sleeper (a range of 1.41 to 1.66). People at the regular end (the 95th percentile) came in at 0.90 (0.81 to 1.00). The authors write that they cannot establish cause and effect. People were 62 on average, and the score counts whether you are in the same state, asleep or awake, at the same clock time on consecutive days. [11]
- Another analysis of the same biobank (60,977 people, 1,859 deaths) found that higher sleep regularity went with 20% to 48% lower risk of dying from any cause, across the top four fifths compared with the least regular fifth. Regularity predicted mortality at least as well as how long people sleep. It is the same group of people, so it is not a second, independent check. [12]
- The size of the effect depends on how regularity is calculated: two widely used open-source calculators gave markedly different scores for the same 70,000+ adults. [13]

### Study (about the breaths)

- A review of 9 small studies (457 people) of slow breathing, 10 breaths a minute or fewer, practiced before bed found that people said they slept longer and better, but the studies that measured sleep with devices were inconclusive. We read the abstract; the full paper is behind a paywall. [22]
- In one randomized study, five minutes a day of cyclic sighing for a month (a double inhale, then a long exhale) lifted mood more than the same time of mindfulness meditation. It was remote, mostly with Stanford students, and it measured mood. Sleep was left out. [23]
- The study asked people to take both breaths in through the nose and let the long one out through the mouth, or to breathe only through the nose if they preferred. [23]
- A review of 12 trials with 785 adults found a small to moderate effect of breathing practices on stress, and urged caution. [24]
- Three breaths is our short version. No study has tested three breaths, or a lock-screen reminder.

### Our experiment {#our-experiment}

Keeping the same clock-time bedtime through the autumn change has not been tested in a study. We offer it as a way to practice steady sleep and wake times. Nobody has shown that it fixes the clock change.

We found no randomized trial that tested a steady bedtime on its own against death or heart outcomes. A few small trials of sleep programs have measured regularity or blood sugar. A search that finds none cannot show there is none.

Seven nights is our choice. The UK study used a 7-day sensor window and a 2021 diary study used 7 days. No clinical threshold sets seven.

A sleep diary, where you write when you went to bed and got up, is a common way to see your own sleep timing. The Consensus Sleep Diary was built by an expert panel and reviewed by groups of good sleepers and people with insomnia; its authors call it a living document that still needs to be tested, refined and validated. [20] [21]

### The clock change itself {#the-clock-change}

In the United States the clocks go back one hour at 2:00 a.m. local time on Sunday 1 November 2026, the first Sunday of November. Sunday 1 November has 25 hours: at 2:00 a.m. the clock goes back to 1:00 a.m. and that hour happens twice. [1] [2] [3] [4]

Most of Europe went back a week earlier, on Sunday 25 October. [5]

Hawaii and most of Arizona do not change their clocks (the Navajo Nation does); neither do American Samoa, Guam, Puerto Rico and the Virgin Islands. [1] [2] [4]

Colombia does not change its clocks: the legal hour is UTC minus 5 all year. [6]

From 1 November, New York (EST, UTC minus 5) and Medellín (UTC minus 5) share the same hour. Before that, New York was one hour ahead. So 20:00 in New York on 3 and 5 November is 20:00 in Medellín and 01:00 UTC the next day. [4] [6]

### The clock and the sun {#the-clock-and-the-sun}

The clock change moves the hands on the wall. The sun stays where it is. The body clock is set mostly by light, and in a large survey, people's sleep on days off followed the sun under standard time but not under summer time. It was a survey of about 55,000 people, from 2007, so it shows an association. [17] [18]

The autumn change is usually gentler than the spring one, and studies disagree on how much. In one study of 50 people followed for 8 weeks around each switch, sleep and activity "readily adjust" to the end of summer time in autumn. A 2025 review of 27 studies found "heterogeneous findings," with the clearest harm in spring and among evening types. We read only its abstract. We do not claim the autumn change hurts your sleep, or how long it takes to settle. [17] [19] [10]

### The moon behind the cards {#the-moon-behind-the-cards}

Each card carries a moon line: how much of the Moon is lit at the hour of that piece, and its phase, here always a waning crescent. The Moon is a fact about the sky. We do not link it to your sleep. [25] [26]

A waning crescent is a morning moon. In Medellín on 3 November it rises at 01:11 and sets at 13:42; in New York it rises at 00:26 and sets at 14:03. At 20:00 it is below the horizon in both cities, so we do not ask you to look for it at night. [25]

The Moon reaches last quarter on Sunday 1 November, the day the clocks go back: 20:28 UTC, which is 15:28 in New York and in Medellín. [25] [26]

The sign on each card is the Western tropical zodiac: twelve signs of 30 degrees, counted from the March equinox, separate from the constellations of the same names. The sidereal zodiac used in India, in its Lahiri form, sits about 24 degrees behind, so its sign is usually a different one. We give the sign as the astrological tradition holds it. Controlled tests of astrology, like a double-blind study in Nature (1985), have not found the effects it claims. [27] [28]

### Where they disagree {#where-they-disagree}

**Does the autumn change hurt sleep?** In one study of 50 people, sleep and activity "readily adjust" to the end of summer time in autumn, though the timing of activity did not adjust to its start in spring. Spain's sleep society writes that the body clock takes several days to readjust and that some people report irritability, poor concentration and insomnia in the first days, without separating autumn from spring. The 2025 review of 27 studies calls its findings heterogeneous and says definitive conclusions are hampered by methodological inconsistencies. We say: gentler in autumn, by an amount nobody has settled. [17] [10] [19]

**Regularity or duration?** The title of the second UK analysis says regularity is "a stronger predictor" than duration; its own comparison gave p between 0.14 and 0.20. We say: at least as well. [12]

**Which end of the night.** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine puts the stress on getting up; the NHS says both, going to bed and getting up. We ask for both and we start with the bedtime, because that is what the tale and the Short are about. [9] [7] [8]

### How we checked this page

On 16 October 2026 we opened the sources above, with the exceptions we say here, and checked each study for retractions (none). We checked the Moon against USNO and JPL Horizons, and the hours with our own calculations. Three reviews are behind a paywall and we read only their abstracts, which we say where they appear. Colombia's decree we read in a copy, because the official site did not open. The EU rule on the clock change we read in a mirror, because EUR-Lex did not open; the date comes from the Dutch government. We crossed each claim with the other sources we could open; where a claim rests on one study, we say so above. Apart from the Moon's sign, there is no tradition to cross-check. For the Moon's sign, the Swiss Ephemeris documentation guides the reading, and the other two sources cross it. Where sources disagree, we say so above.

### What we could not confirm

- A study showing that keeping a steady bedtime through the autumn change changes your health. We found none, and a search that finds nothing cannot show there is nothing.
- That the autumn change harms sleep, and by how much. The studies we read do not agree.
- That three breaths, or a lock-screen reminder, help you sleep. Nobody has measured either.
- That the way regularity is counted changes the scores, from a second, independent group. The finding is one group's.
- The full text of three reviews (Kalkanis, Romigi, Eide). They are behind a paywall.
- The text of Colombia's decree on the official site, and the EU directive on EUR-Lex. We read them in copies.
