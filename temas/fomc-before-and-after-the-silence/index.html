  • The vote: 9 to 3 in July, 12 to 0 in September.
  • The verb: "maintain" in July, "raise" in September.

The Fed's own rule

The silence, which the Fed calls a blackout, is written in a Fed document, the FOMC Policy on External Communications, reaffirmed on 27 January 2026. It runs from 12:00 a.m. ET on the second Saturday before a meeting to 11:59 p.m. ET the day after the meeting. In it, participants, meaning the members and the presidents who do not vote, refrain from giving their views on the economy or on monetary policy in meetings or conversations with the public. The statement and the press conference still come out, and so does data from other agencies. [2] [5] [31] [32]

The same policy lists what is out of place. No one on the Committee is supposed to call the decision before it is announced. That rule holds all year, not only in the silence. [5]

The Philadelphia and Chicago Reserve Banks list the same dates, 17 to 29 October. [6] [7]

The numbers around the range

  • The Fed also sets the rates on its own tools: what it pays on reserves and on overnight reverse repos, and what it charges on overnight repos. Since 17 September: 3.90 percent on reserves, 4.0 on overnight repos, 3.75 on overnight reverse repos. That list comes out with the statement, in the implementation note. The New York Fed posts the effective rate about 9:00 a.m. ET the next business day. [10] [18] [19]
  • The Committee aims for 2 percent inflation over the longer run, measured by the price index for personal consumption expenditures, and for maximum employment. The law names three goals: maximum employment, stable prices and moderate long-term interest rates. "Dual mandate" is the Fed's own phrase. [16] [17]
  • Twelve people vote in 2026: Warsh, Barr, Bowman, Cook, Jefferson, Powell and Waller, who are governors; Williams, president of the New York Fed and Vice Chair of the Committee; and four other regional presidents, Hammack, Kashkari, Logan and Paulson. [4] The statement names who voted against and what they preferred. In July, Hammack, Kashkari and Logan voted against and wanted a quarter-point raise; in April, one member wanted a quarter-point cut. [11] [13]
  • Minutes arrive three weeks after the decision. For this meeting, Wednesday 18 November at 2:00 p.m. ET, which is 2:00 p.m. in Medellín by then. [1] [3] [15]
  • The New York Fed asks dealers and market participants what they expect before each meeting. The October questions are posted. The results come out about three weeks after the meeting, with the minutes. [20]

Shorter statements

The statement ran 341 words in April, 131 in June, 167 in July and 131 in September. At the June meeting, the first under Chair Warsh, the Committee left out forward guidance, which means saying where policy may go next. [9] [11] [12] [13] [23]

Two things move in a statement

A statement has two parts: the decision on the rate and the words about what comes next. Research on data from the 1990s and 2000s associates the words with more movement in longer-term Treasury yields, and the rate decision with the shorter ones. That is an association, in older data, and it does not describe the current Chair. [21] [22]

Two terms get mixed up. A reaction function describes how a central bank adjusts policy in response to changes in the economy. Forward guidance communicates the likely future course of policy. [23]

Where they disagree

The question is whether the Fed's statement tells you where policy may go next or leaves that out. The June minutes say a majority of participants saw advantages in shortening the statement. [14] From the April statement to the June one, a sentence about "the extent and timing of additional adjustments" disappeared. [13] [12]

For leaving it out. Kevin Warsh, Chair of the Fed, at his confirmation hearing, as Brookings quotes him: "I don't believe that I should be previewing for you what a future decision might be." At his press conference on 17 June, in the Fed's transcript: "Financial market prices are probably the most important source of information to guide central bankers." Christine Lagarde, ECB President, on the policy panel of the ECB Forum in Sintra on 1 July 2026, as Brookings quotes her: "if I have one regret, it's to have felt bound and compelled by forward guidance." In its place she proposes what she calls "framework guidance": telling people how the bank comes to its decision. [23] [24] [43] [44]

For a replacement. David Andolfatto, University of Miami, in the AP report carried by Fortune: "I'm with him on dispensing with forward guidance, but you have to replace it with a contingency plan." Michael Feroli, chief U.S. economist at JPMorgan, in a note to clients, as Brookings quotes him: "Chair Warsh gave no forward guidance, but neither did he give present guidance." [23] [24]

Christopher Waller, Fed Governor, said both in a speech in Rome on 6 July 2026: "Forward guidance can help speed up policy transmission, but if it is not flexible enough, it can hinder policy transmission." And: "in some cases, it's best not to use it at all." [42] [23]

In the same report, the AP adds that Warsh "so far hasn't done so", meaning set out how the Fed will react, and that, analysts say, the approach "carries the risk of more violent swings in stock and bond prices." [24]

How the decision gets made does not change with either view: the Committee votes, the statement lists the vote, and the minutes follow three weeks later. We do not say which side is right. The Brookings question stays open.
