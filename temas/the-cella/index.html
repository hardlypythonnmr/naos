The empty cella, the full cella

Why sweep an empty room?

A tale we made up, about a room that is real. And one thing to try in the morning.

The tale

Our tale · Sources [1] [2] [3]

This tale is ours. Its oldest cousin is Euripides' Ion, a boy who sweeps Apollo's temple at Delphi at dawn.

The room is real. The Greeks called it naos and the Romans cella: the inner room of a temple, where the god's image stood.

In our tale, the statue was long gone. The room had one door, and it faced east.

An old keeper swept it every morning. One day a boy asked her:

"Why sweep an empty room?"

"Come back tomorrow," she said. "Early."

He came back in the dark. At sunrise she opened the door. The sun crossed the floor and climbed to where the statue used to stand.

"Every morning the sun comes in," she said. "So every morning, I sweep."

"Go on. Stand there."

He stood in the light.

Years later, he was the one sweeping. One morning a girl asked him why.

He told her to come back tomorrow. Early.

What became of that room, we'll tell you on the last night of the year. Until then, the door stays open.

In the morning

Our experiment · the breathing comes from a longer version that was studied [18]

What the boy did, on your own morning. At first light, any morning. About two minutes.

  1. Open a door. Or the window where the morning comes in. If it's still dark, wait for it.
  2. Sweep one small thing. The doorstep, the table, the corner by your bed. A minute, no more.
  3. Stand in the light and breathe. Breathe in, then in a little more, then out, long and slow. That's one breath. Take three. If you get dizzy, go back to normal breathing.

If you want it in one line: when I open the door in the morning, I take three breaths in the light.

Where this comes from

The tale is ours. The room, the word and the keepers come from the Greeks. For ancient Greek religion, the Greek dictionary scholars use (LSJ) and the published archaeology guide the reading. On the room and its keepers we found no voice from inside that religion; if you practice it, tell us. For the word as it is still used, Greek Orthodox usage speaks from inside.

Study (about the behavior)

  • In one randomized study, five minutes a day of cyclic sighing for a month (a double inhale, then a long exhale) lifted mood more than the same time of mindfulness meditation. It was remote, mostly with Stanford students, and mood was self-reported. [18]
  • The study asked people to take both breaths in through the nose and let the long one out through the mouth, or to breathe only through the nose if they preferred. [18]
  • A review of 12 trials with 785 adults found a small to moderate effect of breathing practices on stress, and urged caution. [19]
  • Three breaths is our short version. We found no study that tested it.

Our experiment

The door at first light, the one small thing swept, the three breaths in the light, the one thing kept from the year: no study we'd stand behind. Writing down good things from your days is popular, but the studies disagree. In one, a placebo did about as well. [20]

The word

Naos is Greek for a temple and, in a narrower sense, for its inmost part, the shrine that held the god's image. In Latin the same room is the cella. English uses both words. [2] [3] [4]

In Athenian prose and inscriptions the word was neōs. Naos is the Doric form, which Athenian tragedy also used, and it is the one that lasted: in Greek today a church is a naos. Orthodoxia, a Greek news agency on church affairs, writes "Ιερός Ναός" (holy naos) before the name of each church. [2] [5]

In Greek it sounds like nah-OHS. English dictionaries give NAY-os. [6] [4]

The room

Archaeologists find that most Greek temples face the eastern horizon, mostly within the arc where the sun rises. [7] [8]

A 2025 study of its light finds the Parthenon's cella dim inside. Daylight came mostly through the great east door; side windows lit the walls, not the statue. [9]

The same study suggests that on a few mornings a year, one near Athena's great summer festival, the rising sun came straight through the east door and lit the lower half of her statue. [9] [10]

That statue, of gold and ivory and nearly 12 m tall, did not survive antiquity. It disappeared, and copies made in Roman times show what it looked like. We don't show it here. [11] [12]

The keepers

Dictionaries of ancient Greek name the keepers of temples neokoroi. The word probably meant temple-sweeper. In Greek today the neokoros is the church sacristan, who keeps it clean and lights the lamps. [2] [13]

Women kept temples too. In a book on Greek priestesses, Joan Breton Connelly, following earlier work by Alexander Mantis, reads the temple key as the most distinctive sign of a priestess in sculpture and vase painting. A review of the book warns that a significant number of the figures it discusses could show either priestesses or worshippers performing rites. [14] [15]

The oldest cousin

Scholars date Euripides' play Ion to between about 418 and 412 BCE, with no agreement on the year. In it, young Ion serves at Apollo's temple in Delphi. As the sun rises, he cleans the entrance with laurel branches and water, chases the birds away and sweeps the god's pavement, which he calls his daily service. We found him after we made our keeper. [1]

Where they disagree

Which way the door faced. Hannah, Magli and Orlando, on the temples of Akragas in Sicily, write that most Greek temples face east, and also that eastern orientation "is not the universal key". Boutsikas, with 107 temples, finds more variety than one rule explains. Vitruvius, a Roman architect, wrote the opposite rule: if nothing stood in the way, the statue should look west, so the people at the altar would face east. [7] [8] [16]

What the keeper did. LSJ: probably temple-sweeper. The Suda, an old Greek lexicon, says the one who adorns the temple, and not the one who sweeps it. [2] [13]

The sun on the statue. One study, built on a model, and only the lower half of the statue. Oxford's press note sounds bigger than the paper; we follow the paper. In our tale the sun comes in every morning. At the Parthenon, by that study, it reached the statue on a few. [9] [10]

Who the room was for. In ancient Greek religion the room was the house of the image, and the rites took place outside, in front of the temple. In Orthodox Christianity the nave is, in the words of the Orthodox Church in America, "the place of the Church understood as the assembly and people of God." It's our tale: we don't say the Greeks filled the cella. [2] [7] [17]

On 16 October 2026 we opened every source above. We checked the Sun and the Moon against USNO and JPL Horizons, and each study for retractions (none). For the words and the room, LSJ and the published archaeology guide the reading. We crossed each claim with the other sources we could open; where a claim rests on one study or one book, we say so above. For ancient Greek religion we found no voice from inside on these points. Where sources disagree, we say so above.

What we could not confirm

  • A Greek speaker saying naos for us. We have dictionaries only.
  • That morning daylight steadies the body clock and sleep: two sources, not three. The door at first light stays our experiment.
  • Other ancient tales of a temple sweeper besides Ion: we haven't searched further yet.
