## 2 · Try it {#try-it}

Our method · Study [1] [4]

You don't have to learn anything. Pick something from today that made you react: a headline, a post, a forwarded message.

A closed case: in March 2009, obituaries of the composer Maurice Jarre printed a quote as his own words. [37] [38]

[Figure: the three moves. The steps below are the figure's own text, printed once, inside the figure. Below 1000 px the drawings stay folded behind "Watch it once"; the text is always there. Under the figure, one fold, "About this figure": inside, the line "What this shows" and the still version (no motion, no script).]

**1 · Name them.** Write one line: who is telling you this? An outlet, a person, a company, or a forward with no name.
*Jarre:* several obituaries, all with the same quote.

**2 · Leave the page.** Open a new tab and search that name or that sentence. Who runs it? Who pays for it? Who else reports the same thing?
*Jarre:* trace the quote back and you reach Jarre's Wikipedia page, where a 22-year-old student had added it just after Jarre died.

**3 · Find the original, then look again from another side.** The document, the data, the full quote. Then someone who says otherwise, in their own words. Ask who gains if you believe it as it stands.
*Jarre:* there was no original. The student made the quote up, and the Guardian ran a correction.

That's it. You can say it this way: "When something makes me react, I ask who is telling me before I pass it on."

## 4 · Where this comes from {#where-this-comes-from}

*Why, and how to read it (folded; inside, each part opens on its own)*

The tradition behind this page is a trade: people who check for a living. No sacred text or tradition is told here.

### The trade
*Who does this for a living, and what rules they publish.*

Mike Caulfield published SIFT: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace the claim to the original. University libraries teach it. He wrote *Verified* with Wineburg, of Stanford's Civic Online Reasoning group. [1] [2] [3]

Newsrooms publish their own rules. Reuters promises independence, integrity and freedom from bias. The AP lists standards on accuracy and conflicts of interest. A rule on paper tells you what to hold them to. Whether they met it is something you check. [12] [13]

Fact-checkers who sign the code of the International Fact-Checking Network promise to be nonpartisan and open about sources, funding and method, and to publish corrections. Colombiacheck, from Colombia, shows the signatory seal on its site. On 16 October 2026 the network's own directory listed it as in renewal, last verified in March 2024. [14] [15]

Since this page asks who is telling you: Google.org, Google's charitable arm, supported Stanford's Civic Online Reasoning curriculum and paid for the district study described below. [16] A 2024 correction to the Hoes paper declared funding from Facebook/Meta and one author's commercial affiliation. [7]

### What the studies say
*What happens when people check, and when they don't. Lateral reading means leaving the page to check who is behind it.*

- A headline can mislead without one false word in it. In experiments, misleading headlines changed what readers remembered, even when the article under them was accurate. [8]
- Fact-checkers against historians and students: 45 people, 10 historians with doctorates, 10 fact-checkers, 25 Stanford students. The fact-checkers opened new tabs to see who was behind a site, and got to a sounder answer, faster. Experts against novices, so it shows a difference. It cannot show what caused it. [4]
- In a US study of 3,446 high school students, 96% never found out that a site posing as a source of facts on climate science was tied to the fossil fuel industry. Two thirds could not tell a news story from an ad. US students only. [5]
- Teaching it helped. In one US school district, 271 students who had six 50-minute lessons in lateral reading improved more than 228 in regular classes. A Swedish team got the same direction with 209 students. Both were measured at the end of the course. [6]
- The more often you meet a claim, the truer it feels, even when you know better. [9]
- Stopping to ask whether a headline is accurate makes people a bit more careful about what they share. The effect is small. [11]
- A correction often helps. It seldom erases the first claim. [10]
- Doubt can go too far. In experiments in three countries, tips and fact-checks made people believe false claims less, and true ones less too. When your doubt has done its work, trust the better source. [7]
- Who pays matters. A Cochrane review of 75 papers found that drug and device studies paid for by their makers reported favorable efficacy results about 1.27 times as often, and favorable conclusions 1.34 times as often. That covers drugs and devices only. In 1965 a sugar industry group paid for a review of the research without saying so, according to a 2016 analysis of the industry's internal documents. The review came out in 1967 and played down sugar's role in heart disease. [17] [18]

### AI and the news
*How well AI tools name a source, and how people use them.*

- In a test published in March 2025, eight AI search tools were shown a passage from a news article and asked for its source. More than 60% of the answers were wrong. One tool was wrong 37% of the time, another 94%. They often cited copied or syndicated versions, and for two of the tools more than half the links were made up or broken. These are the tools of that month. [23]
- In a study published in February 2025, the BBC tested four AI assistants on 100 news questions. BBC journalists rated 51% of the answers as having significant problems. In 19% of the answers that cited BBC content, the assistant added factual errors, and 13% of the quotes were altered or missing. The tests ran in December 2024, and the BBC was testing its own content. [24]
- In a study published in October 2025, 22 public broadcasters in 18 countries checked more than 3,000 AI answers about the news. 45% had at least one significant problem, 31% had sourcing problems, and 20% had major accuracy problems. We read these numbers in a press release from VRT, a member of the European Broadcasting Union; the Union's own report page did not open for us. [25]
- An Apple notification summary put a false claim about a man in a BBC story on iPhone screens. The BBC complained, and in January 2025 Apple paused news summaries. [26] [27]
- In the Reuters Institute's 2026 survey, 10% of people use AI chatbots for news each week, up from 7%, and 16% of people under 35. In 27 markets, 4% of all respondents say they always or often click the sources in an AI answer, against 19% for search engines. Among people who use chatbots for news, 42% say they do. This is what people say they do. [28]

### Trust and tiredness
*How much people trust the news, and what too much of it is linked with.*

- In September 2026, 33% of US adults told Gallup they have a great deal or fair amount of trust in newspapers, TV and radio, up from a record low of 28% in 2025. It edged up, and the margin is 4 points. Across 48 markets, 37% trust most news most of the time, the lowest since the series began in 2015. In Colombia it is 25%, and 60% worry about what is real and what is false online. [29] [28]
- Doomscrolling is linked with anxiety and distress. The review that found the link, 44 studies, rates its certainty as very low and says it does not establish what causes what. Also, 42% of people worldwide say they avoid the news sometimes or often, 49% in Colombia. One review, published in September 2026. [30] [28]

### Where they disagree
*Two disputes, each side in its own words.*

**Google and Pew.** Pew: with an AI summary on the page, people clicked a regular result in 8% of visits, against 15% without one. Google, 6 August 2025: "total organic click volume from Google Search to websites has been relatively stable year-over-year." The post gives no figures. About the Pew study, a Google spokesperson said: "This study uses a flawed methodology and skewed query-set that is not representative of Search traffic." [31] [32] [33]

**Türkiye and Australia.** Türkiye's Murat Kurum, COP31 President, said on 10 September: "the final decision lies with the presidency of COP31" (English translation, as Climate Home reports it). Australia's prime minister's office said in November 2025 that Australia is "President of Negotiations" with "exclusive authority in relation to the negotiations". [35] [36]

**Doubt and trust.** Hoes and colleagues wrote that these interventions "may increase scepticism towards all information, including accurate information." Altay and colleagues wrote that literacy tips "typically encourage people to be skeptical of the news despite the small prevalence of false news in Western democracies." The two teams share an author, so they are not independent of each other. [7]

### More, if you want it
*Three older findings, one line each.*

- You can look up who owns an outlet. For Colombia, Reporters Without Borders mapped it in 2015: more than 200 radio stations, 50 TV channels and 50 newspapers, with high concentration in TV and radio. These are 2015 data. [19]
- In a UK sample of news from 2006, much of it leaned on agency copy and press releases. In health news, exaggeration in stories went together with exaggeration in the press release. That alone does not show which caused which. [20] [21]
- A 2016 study estimated that 59% of links to five big news sites mentioned on Twitter were never clicked. It counts links. Some people may have read the story anyway. One study. [22]

### Our experiment

The two questions and the three moves are ours. They echo what fact-checkers do, and the match is our reading. We have not timed the step, so we call it a short step, and we have not measured whether it helps you. The prompt asks only where to look, because the tests above show assistants get sources wrong with confidence.

Should every image made with AI carry a label?
Official data · Study [41] [43] [47] · Voice: synthetic · naos/s/1020p

*More on this piece (folded)*

What the rule says, in its own words, and what researchers measured about labels; we found no named voice against them. One hard question, and one thing to ask the youngest person you know.

We found no named person or group arguing against labels. So this card gives the rule in its own words and then what researchers measured.

How the decision gets made. The EU's AI Act, Article 50, applies from 2 August 2026: providers must mark AI-generated audio, images, video and text in a machine-readable way, and whoever uses an AI system to make a deepfake must say it was artificially generated or manipulated. An EU amendment in force since 27 July 2026 gave AI systems already on the market until 2 December 2026 to add the marks. The rule on deepfakes kept its date. [41] [44]
Elsewhere, briefly. China has required explicit and hidden marks on AI-generated content since 1 September 2025. [45] California's AI Transparency Act has been in effect since 2 August 2026 for large AI image, video and audio generators; we read law-firm summaries, because the statute did not open for us. [46]

**The rule, in its own words.** The EU's AI Act: AI-generated content is "increasingly hard for humans to distinguish from human-generated and authentic content", and it brings "new risks of misinformation and manipulation at scale, fraud, impersonation and consumer deception." The Commission says Article 50 is meant to "address risks of deception and manipulation, fostering the integrity of the information ecosystem." [43] [42]

**What researchers found.** A plain "made with AI" label did little to change whether people would engage with a misleading image. [47] When only some false stories carry a warning, the ones without it look more trustworthy. [48]
Hidden watermarks are a different tool from a visible label. One team found that "pixel-level invisible watermarks are vulnerable to this regeneration attack." The same team adds that watermarks that keep the image semantically similar "can be an alternative defense". [49]

Hard question: who enforces a label that a forger can skip?
Try this: ask the youngest person you know how they tell whether an image is real. On your own, write down how you tell, then ask someone later.
